Immediate danger or possible risk to life: call 999 when it is needed, stay with the child, and summon the DSL or a deputy. Don't delay action to work through the steps. National Context

In July 2024 the National Police Chiefs' Council and the College of Policing described violence against women and girls as a national emergency. On 18 December 2025 the Government published Freedom from violence and abuse, its cross-government VAWG strategy, with the ambition of halving VAWG within a decade, and describes VAWG as a national emergency. The strategy places education at the centre of prevention and early intervention.

KCSIE 2026 adds content on VAWG and misogyny, retitles child-on-child abuse to include harassment and violence, and asks schools to recognise the escalatory nature of misogyny and the benefits of early identification.

Scenario Overview

This scenario addresses misogynistic attitudes and behaviours toward female students and staff, including sexist language, sexual harassment, objectification, and gender-based violence. It applies KCSIE 2026 and the Government's VAWG strategy, Freedom from violence and abuse (December 2025), through the RESPOND workflow to provide comprehensive guidance for educational settings.

Critical Understanding

Misogyny is often normalised as "banter" or dismissed as "boys being boys." KCSIE 2026 is clear that sexual harassment and sexual violence should never be passed off as "banter", "just having a laugh", "a part of growing up" or "boys being boys", and that failing to challenge it can lead to a culture that normalises abuse. This scenario addresses attitudes, behaviours, and language that demonstrate contempt, prejudice, or hostility toward girls and women, recognising that:

  • Misogyny can escalate to sexual harassment and violence
  • It intersects with other forms of discrimination (race, disability, sexual orientation, faith)
  • Online influence (misogynistic influencers, the "manosphere", incel culture) is a growing concern, and can harm boys and young men as well as girls
  • Cultural sensitivity must be balanced with child protection obligations
  • This is about systemic culture change, not just individual incidents

Balance. VAWG is gendered, and KCSIE recognises girls are more likely to be victims. Anyone can experience sexual violence or harassment, including boys and lesbian, gay, bisexual or gender-questioning students, who may find it harder to report. Most boys and young men are respectful. The aim is to challenge misogynistic ideas and behaviour, not to stigmatise boys as a group (Freedom from violence and abuse; RSHE statutory guidance).

How RESPOND Works in This Scenario

RESPOND is powered by ACT: Active Intervention (inaction is not neutral), Contextual Safeguarding (harm happens in peer groups, places and online spaces) and Trauma-Informed Practice (safety before emotion before thinking). The RESPOND Helix: How the Steps Move

▼

RESPOND is taught in a line, Recognise to Document, because sequence gives structure under pressure. In practice it moves as a helix. The first dimension is the steps themselves: you may loop back, hold at a step, or go straight to Notify. The second is time and severity: each turn sits higher as a concern develops. The third is context: a concern involving several children, places or platforms traces a wider spiral.

Relational and Systemic

For a child, safeguarding is relational: it happens through people they trust. For the adults around them, it is also systemic: the steps, records and shared decisions that make sure what a child tells one person reaches the people who can help.

Relational is why children tell. Systemic is why telling works.

The RESPOND steps follow the same shape: Recognise, Engage and Support are relational; Pause is the bridge; Offer, Notify and Document are the systems steps. Neither works alone. Relationship without the system becomes secrecy, which is why staff never promise to keep a disclosure to themselves. The system without relationship becomes process that children do not trust enough to use. When you share information, be open with the child about what you are sharing and why.

Sequence

How RESPOND is taught.

  1. The seven steps: RESPOND is taught in this order, Recognise to Document, because sequence gives structure under pressure. Recognise, Engage and Support are the relational steps; Pause is the bridge; Offer, Notify and Document are the systems steps.
  2. But practice is not a line: In practice a concern rarely moves in a straight line. You may loop back, hold at a step, or go straight to Notify. Choose one of the examples to see how the steps move.
Boy displaying misogyny

A Year 10 boy whose misogynistic comments escalate over a term. He is a child too: the helix wraps around him and the girls affected.

  1. Turn 1: a comment in class: In a lesson, a Year 10 boy says women "shouldn't be in charge" and that girls who disagree are "hysterical". Some classmates laugh. Recognise: it is aimed at the girls in the room, and one is visibly uncomfortable.
  2. Turn 1: Engage: Direct, calmly, if it is safe to do so: "That's not how we talk about people here." Later, privately, TED: "Tell me where you've heard that."
  3. Turn 1: Support: check in briefly with the girls affected. Connection before correction applies to him as well.
  4. Turn 1: Pause: Three Lenses. Low risk for now, but it echoes content he may be watching online. Do not get drawn into a debate about the ideology.
  5. Turn 1: Notify: pass it to the DSL as soon as is practicably possible, through the school's usual system. Offer can wait for this turn.
  6. Turn 1: Document: GOLD, with his exact words and who heard them. This record is what lets the next turn begin higher up.
  7. Turn 2: a named girl (two weeks later): A girl tells her tutor he has been commenting on girls' bodies in a group chat and calling her names. Because the lesson was recorded, the DSL can link the two. Recognise: this is now sexual harassment, with a named girl affected.
  8. Turn 2: Support her first: believe her, safety first, and ask what would help her feel safe in lessons and at break.
  9. Turn 2: Pause: Three Lenses. Her safety and wellbeing; his rights and needs as a child too. Do not investigate or ask to see the chat.
  10. Turn 2: N before O: Notify: the DSL, today. The DSL decides the response under the school's child-on-child abuse procedures. If in doubt, N before O.
  11. Turn 2: Offer, once the DSL has decided: education and consequences for him; support, and a say in what happens next, for her. Model the alternative: respect is expected, not optional.
  12. Turn 2: loop back: Loop back to Engage with him. The relationship still matters: name the behaviour, not the boy, and keep the door open.
  13. Turn 2: Document: GOLD for each conversation, recorded separately for each student.
  14. Turn 3: risk: He shares an image of a girl in his year that has been altered to look sexual. Recognise: this is a child protection matter.
  15. Turn 3: straight to Notify: Go straight from Recognise to Notify: tell the DSL immediately. Do not view, copy, forward or delete the image.
  16. Turn 3: Support: the girl may not know yet. The DSL decides how and when she and her parents are told, and what support she needs.
  17. Turn 3: Document: GOLD, as soon as is practicably possible. Describe what you were told, never the image itself.
Girl experiencing misogyny

A Year 9 girl on the receiving end of misogyny, over a term. Each turn is higher: the same steps, at greater urgency.

  1. Turn 1: "just banter": A Year 9 girl laughs off being called "a 6 out of 10" by boys at break: "it's just banter". Recognise: many girls say this happens so often it is not worth reporting.
  2. Turn 1: Engage: TED. "Tell me what happens at break." Don't minimise it, and don't push her.
  3. Turn 1: Support: tell her it isn't OK and it isn't her fault, and that she can come back to you at any time.
  4. Turn 1: Notify: pass it to the DSL, even though she says she's fine. Patterns are only visible when things are passed on.
  5. Turn 1: Document: GOLD, with her exact words.
  6. Turn 2: a pattern (three weeks later): Her PE teacher notices she has stopped changing for PE and sits alone. Because the first comment was recorded, the DSL can see the link.
  7. Turn 2: Engage: a trusted adult checks in privately. She says boys have been rating girls' bodies in the corridor outside the changing rooms.
  8. Turn 2: loop back: Loop back to Recognise: new information changes the picture. This is sexual harassment, and it is affecting her.
  9. Turn 2: Support: safety first. Ask what would help: another place to change, a trusted adult, a plan for break times.
  10. Turn 2: Pause: Three Lenses. Her rights include a say in what happens next, but you cannot promise to keep it secret.
  11. Turn 2: N before O: Notify before Offer: the DSL, as soon as is practicably possible.
  12. Turn 2: Offer, once the DSL has decided: work out with her what support she wants and what she would like to happen. Model the alternative: she should not have to change her life to avoid them.
  13. Turn 2: Document: GOLD, with dates and what each adult has noticed.
  14. Turn 3: disclosure: Weeks later she tells you a boy has been pressuring her to send nude images and says he will spread a rumour if she doesn't.
  15. Turn 3: Engage: listen, and use the disclosure script: "I believe you, and I'm taking what you've told me seriously. This is not your fault. I'm going to help keep you safe, and I'll explain what happens next."
  16. Turn 3: straight to Notify: Go straight to Notify: the DSL, immediately. Do not ask to see any messages or images.
  17. Turn 3: Support: stay with her while help comes, and be honest that you have to pass this on.
  18. Turn 3: Document: GOLD, her exact words, as soon as is practicably possible.
Wider spiral

One incident, several people at once: a Year 10 group chat rating girls. Each strand is its own spiral.

  1. Three strands: A girl tells you boys in a Year 10 group chat are rating girls in the year. A single-child concern traces a tight spiral; this one is wider: the girls rated, the boys who posted, and everyone else in the chat.
  2. At the same time: Support the girls who have been rated, while engaging the boys who posted. The chat is still being recognised: is it active, and who else is in it?
  3. At the same time: Pause for each person. The chat goes straight to the DSL: do not view, screenshot, forward or save anything.
  4. Joined up: Notify: one joined-up picture to the DSL, who coordinates the response and any contact with parents or other agencies.
  5. Different speeds: The strands move at different speeds. Education and consequences for the boys follow the DSL's decisions; bystanders are shown how to speak up next time, if it is safe to do so. Every strand ends in Document.
When it breaks

The same Year 9 girl, when steps are skipped.

  1. Turn 1: The same girl laughs off being rated at break. Recognise happens.
  2. Pause skipped: The adult agrees it is "just banter" and moves on. Pause is skipped: the adult reacts to how she presents, not to what is happening.
  3. Document skipped: Nothing is recorded. When her PE teacher later notices she has stopped changing, there is nothing to connect it to. The pattern stays invisible.
  4. Notify skipped: The concern stays with one adult. Professionals working in isolation, and information not being shared, are recurring findings in serious case reviews.
  5. The helix fragments: The structure that should wrap around her falls apart. She learns that telling changes nothing, and stops telling. That is how harm escalates unseen.
  6. Put it back together: Every step taken puts the structure back. Relational is why children tell; systemic is why telling works.

R RECOGNISE: Identify the Concern

▼

Notice physical, emotional, behavioural and contextual cues of misogyny and VAWG. Trust your instincts: you don't need certainty to act.

The 4 Ps Context Scan

People
Who is involved? Power dynamics? A group, an older student, a friendship or relationship?
Places
Where is it happening? Changing rooms, corridors, toilets, sports fields, boarding houses, transport
Platforms
Digital element? Group chats, social media, AI tools, image sharing
Patterns

The Escalation of Unseen Harm: How Misogyny Escalates

KCSIE 2026 asks schools to recognise the escalatory nature of misogyny. RESPOND's Escalation of Unseen Harm shows how risk accumulates when early indicators are not acted on. Each stage is a missed opportunity; the earliest is the easiest to interrupt.

  1. Micro-indicators: sexist "jokes" and "banter" go unchallenged; a girl stops contributing in class
  2. Pattern formation: repeated comments, "rating" of girls, a group chat with misogynistic content; the targeted student starts avoiding certain people or places
  3. Visible distress: anxiety, avoiding PE or changing rooms, changes in dress or attendance; no disclosure yet
  4. Significant harm (unspoken): ongoing harassment, images shared or threatened; no one has asked or created a safe space to tell
  5. Crisis event: sexual assault, wide distribution of images, or an acute mental health crisis
  6. System breakdown: school refusal, leaving the school, police involvement, entrenched harm

The Response Threshold: the moment you notice but hesitate is the moment the trajectory can be interrupted.

Impact on Targeted Students

Immediate Effects

  • Anxiety, hypervigilance, fear of school spaces
  • Academic disengagement or decline
  • Self-censorship and reduced participation
  • Changes in dress or behaviour to avoid attention
  • Social isolation or withdrawal
  • Avoidance of specific individuals, locations, or activities

Longer-Term Consequences

  • Reduced aspirations and confidence
  • Mental health difficulties (depression, anxiety, PTSD)
  • Internalised misogyny and self-objectification
  • Acceptance of harmful relationship dynamics
  • Career limitations and subject choices affected
  • Normalisation of gender-based violence

Questions to Consider at Recognition Stage

  • Pattern or Isolated: Is this an isolated incident or pattern of behaviour?
  • Multiple Involvement: Are multiple students involved or targeted?
  • Digital Component: Is there online component requiring digital evidence preservation?
  • Intersectionality: Does this intersect with other protected characteristics (race, disability, sexual orientation, faith)?
  • Immediate Safety: Is there immediate safety risk to any student?
  • Radicalisation: Is there any sign the student may be being drawn into supporting terrorism or terrorist violence? Misogyny alone does not meet this threshold

Documentation at Recognition Stage

Immediate Recording Requirements

  • Date, time, location of incident(s)
  • Specific language or actions observed (verbatim quotes)
  • Names of individuals involved (victim(s) and alleged perpetrator(s))
  • Witnesses present
  • Where any online content is (platform, account, date). Never view, copy or forward nude or semi-nude images; for other online harassment, follow DSL direction on preservation
  • Immediate context and circumstances
  • Your immediate response/actions taken

E ENGAGE: Communicate Effectively

▼

Respond with calm, compassionate listening and create felt safety. Challenge what you witness, if it is safe to do so.

Witnessed Behaviour or a Disclosure? Two Different Responses

  • You witness misogynistic language or behaviour: use Active Intervention and the 4Ds, if it is safe to do so. Inaction is not neutral.
  • A student discloses harassment or abuse: listen and support. Do not confront or question the alleged perpetrator. Do not investigate. Notify the DSL, who decides what happens next.

The 4Ds of Active Intervention

Direct
Address it, if it is safe: "Stop. That comment is sexist."
Distract
Redirect: interrupt, change the activity, call the targeted student away
Delegate
Get help: another adult, on-call, the DSL
Delay
Act later, safely: check in afterwards with the student who was targeted, and tell the DSL

If Observing Misogynistic Behaviour

Immediate Intervention Steps (If It Is Safe to Do So)

Your safety, and the safety of the student being targeted, come first. Only intervene directly if it is safe to do so. If it is not safe (for example a group, a risk of violence, or you are alone), do not confront: keep your distance, get another adult or use the on-call system, call 999 if anyone is in immediate danger, then record what you saw and tell the DSL.

  1. Interrupt promptly, if it is safe to do so: "Stop. That language/behaviour is not acceptable."
  2. Name the issue clearly: "What you just said/did is misogynistic and creates an unsafe environment."
  3. State expectations: "In this school, we treat everyone with dignity and respect regardless of gender."
  4. Avoid debating: Don't engage in arguments about whether something is "really" misogyny.

If Receiving a Disclosure

VAWG-Responsive Communication

  1. Thank the student: "Thank you for telling me. That took courage."
  2. Believe them and take it seriously: "I believe you, and I'm taking what you've told me seriously. This is not your fault. I'm going to help keep you safe, and I'll explain what happens next." (KCSIE 2026: victims should be reassured they are being taken seriously and will be supported and kept safe.)
  3. Use TED, never "why": "Tell me what happened." "Explain what you mean." "Describe what that felt like." Avoid "why" questions, leading questions and expressing shock. TED is for understanding, not investigating: ask only what you need to pass the concern on, and never ask a child to describe or show intimate images (UKCIS)
  4. Validate feelings: "I can understand why this is upsetting. This was not your fault."
  5. Use their words: Reflect back what they have said without labelling it for them. The DSL, and police where relevant, decide how it is classified. Do not ask leading questions.
  6. Affirm their rights: "You have a right to feel safe and respected at school."
  7. Explain safeguarding duty: "I need to report this to keep you and others safe, but I'll explain what happens next."
  8. Offer choices where possible: KCSIE 2026 says victims should be given as much control as reasonably possible over how the matter proceeds and what support they receive, balanced against the duty to protect others

▼

Sharing Information: the Statutory Duty (from 30 September 2026)

In school, concerns go to the DSL, who leads on sharing with other agencies. Under the Information Sharing Duty (section 16LA of the Children Act 2004), information relevant to a child's safety or welfare must be shared with other safeguarding agencies where it may help them. There is no threshold to meet: this includes early help, unmet need and situations where a child may pose a risk to others.

The three questions the statutory guidance asks:

  • Is it relevant? Does it bear on the child's safety or welfare? This can include information about another child or adult connected to them.
  • Could it help? Could it help the other agency assess need, make a decision, provide support or take action?
  • Would sharing do more harm than not sharing? This is rare: for example, where sharing would put the child at immediate risk or prejudice a police investigation. Lack of consent, a parent's objection, fear of complaint, feeling unsure, or data protection are not reasons not to share. If you are unsure, ask the DSL (or the Data Protection Officer on data protection) straight away.

How to share well:

  • Share what is relevant and necessary: a summary rather than a whole file, and no stigmatising labels or untested allegations unless they are relevant.
  • Check your assumptions: would the same information about a different child be seen in the same way?
  • Be open with the young person and family, unless that would increase the risk: explain what will be shared, with whom and why. Don't ask permission if the information will be shared anyway; that offers a false choice. Acknowledge their worries, and keep their wishes and feelings in view.
  • Record the decision to share, or not to share, and the reasons.

Information Sharing Duty statutory guidance (September 2026), paragraphs 11 to 14, 18, 21, 33 and 42 to 45. Research supports being open: fear of privacy breaches is one of the main reasons young people do not tell professionals what is happening to them (Firmin et al., Beyond Risk in Adolescent Safeguarding (2026), p. 58).

Legal Considerations

Statutory Framework

▼

  • Working Together to Safeguard Children 2026: Multi-agency arrangements, including Family Help

  • Equality Act 2010 (Part 6): Schools must not discriminate against or harass students because of sex. A school's response to sex-based harassment between students must itself be non-discriminatory; take legal advice on specific cases
  • Equality Act 2010 (as amended by the Worker Protection Act 2023 and the Employment Rights Act 2025): Employers must take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of staff; from 30 October 2026 this becomes all reasonable steps, and employers must not permit harassment of staff by third parties, which in a school includes students and parents
  • Public Sector Equality Duty (s149): Applies to state-funded schools; independent schools are not bound by it
  • RSHE statutory guidance (July 2025, in force September 2026): Teaching on consent, healthy relationships, misogyny and incel culture
  • Anti-bullying duty: Independent School Standards, Part 3, paragraph 10 (an effective anti-bullying strategy, implemented so far as reasonably practicable; DfE guidance expects it to address cyberbullying and prejudice-based bullying); maintained schools: Education and Inspections Act 2006, section 89

When the Target Is a Member of Staff

▼

Misogyny in schools is not only directed at students. Female teachers and support staff are also targeted by students, online and in person. This section applies RESPOND when the person harmed is an adult in the school.

Two Routes, Running Together

  • The student's behaviour is a behaviour and, where indicated, a safeguarding matter. It is recorded on the school's system and the DSL considers what it says about the student and the wider culture.
  • The harm to the member of staff is a staff wellbeing and employment matter. Her line manager and HR are responsible for her support and for the school's duties as employer.

One route does not replace the other. A member of staff should never be left to manage the consequences alone because the student is "just a child", and a student's safeguarding needs are not set aside because the victim is an adult.

The School's Duty as Employer

Since 26 October 2024 the Equality Act 2010, as amended by the Worker Protection (Amendment of Equality Act 2010) Act 2023, has required employers to take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their employees, anticipating the risk before it happens.

From 30 October 2026 the Employment Rights Act 2025 strengthens this:

  • Employers must take all reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment
  • Employers must not permit harassment of their employees by third parties. In a school, third parties include students, parents and visitors. The school can be liable for such harassment unless it has taken all reasonable steps to prevent it

This is a proactive duty on the school: risk assessment, clear policy, training and a consistent leadership response. It is not something an individual teacher is expected to secure for herself. Take HR or legal advice on individual cases.

R: Recognise

What It Can Look Like

▼
  • Sexualised or degrading comments about a teacher's body, clothing or private life
  • Challenges to authority framed by sex: "I don't take orders from a woman", refusing instructions from female staff but not male staff
  • "Rating" staff, noises, gestures, or comments made just loud enough to be heard
  • Misogynistic influencer phrases directed at staff, or content shared in class to provoke
  • Group behaviour: a class or friendship group coordinating disruption against one female teacher
  • Online: fake accounts, posts, memes or group-chat content about a member of staff
  • Covert photos or videos of staff, and AI-altered or "deepfake" sexual images of staff
  • Harassment from parents, or from colleagues (see Notify below: this follows a different route)

Why It Is Often Missed

  • Staff feel reporting it will look like they cannot manage their classes
  • Comments are dismissed as "testing boundaries" or treated as low-level disruption
  • Each incident seems too small to report, so the pattern is never seen
  • Early career, supply, support and international staff may be targeted more and report less

Being targeted is not a sign of weak classroom management. Leaders should say this explicitly. Nearly a quarter (23.4%) of female teachers reported misogyny from a pupil in the last year, up from 17.4% in 2023 (NASUWT survey of over 5,000 teachers, 2026). Teachers report misogynistic comments after addressing behaviour, and being ignored by male pupils because they are female.

E: Engage

In the Moment (If You Are the Target)

  • If it is safe to do so, name it briefly and calmly: "That comment is sexist and it is not acceptable. We'll deal with it after the lesson."
  • If you feel unsafe or the situation is escalating, do not challenge further: use the on-call system and get support
  • Don't debate it in front of the class
  • Use the on-call or removal system if the lesson cannot continue safely
  • Note the exact words as soon as you can
  • You do not have to deal with the follow-up yourself

If You Are a Colleague Who Witnesses It

  • Don't leave it for her to challenge: if it is safe to do so, step in and name it; if not, get help
  • Don't laugh along or let it pass as a joke
  • Don't take over in a way that undermines her authority; support, then check in privately
  • Report what you saw: a witness account strengthens the record
  • Male colleagues challenging sexist behaviour towards female colleagues sends a clear message to students

P: Pause

  • Is this one incident or a pattern? Check whether other female staff have reported the same student or group.
  • What is driving it? The student is still a child. Online influence, home, peer pressure or their own experience of harm may sit behind the behaviour, and the DSL should consider this.
  • Is there a risk to her safety? Threats, following, or knowledge of her home or personal accounts raise the level of response.
  • Regulate before responding. Being targeted is personal. It is reasonable to step back before deciding what to do next.

Do Not

  • Search a student's phone yourself. Senior leaders act under the DfE Searching, screening and confiscation guidance
  • View, copy or forward intimate or sexual images of the member of staff (including altered or deepfake images); note where they are and report. For other online content about her, agree with a senior leader how evidence is preserved
  • Contact the student's parents yourself before agreeing the approach with a senior leader
  • Respond to the content online

O: Offer

  • For the student: a clear consequence under the behaviour policy, plus education on the impact of misogyny. Consequence and education sit together, not as alternatives.
  • For the member of staff: choices about her involvement, a follow-up review date, and a clear outcome communicated to her.
  • For the class or year group: where behaviour is collective, a planned response led by senior staff, not left to the targeted teacher.

N: Notify

Who to Tell, Depending on Who Is Responsible

▼

A student:

  • Record on the school's system as soon as is practicably possible and tell your line manager or head of year
  • Tell the DSL where there are images, threats, sexual content, online abuse, a group dimension, or concerns about the student's own welfare
  • Police via a senior leader where there are threats, stalking, or sharing or threatening to share intimate images (including altered images)

A parent or visitor:

  • Senior leadership and HR; apply the school's policy on unacceptable behaviour by parents and visitors

A colleague:

  • HR, under the dignity at work or grievance procedure
  • If the behaviour also raises any concern about how that colleague behaves towards children, it is a safeguarding matter: follow KCSIE Part 4 (low-level concerns, or the headteacher and the Local Authority Designated Officer (LADO) where the harm threshold may be met)
  • If the concern is about the headteacher, report to the chair of governors or proprietor

D: Document

  • Date, time, location, exact words, and who was present
  • Where online content is (platform, account, date) without copying images
  • Action taken with the student, and the support offered to the member of staff
  • The outcome communicated back to her
  • Incidents against staff recorded in a way that lets leaders see patterns by sex, role and year group

For Leaders

  • Review incidents against staff termly, alongside student incidents
  • Include staff experience of misogyny in staff surveys
  • Check the school's risk assessment and policies show all reasonable steps taken to prevent sexual harassment of staff, including by students and parents (the standard from 30 October 2026)
  • A pattern of female staff being targeted is evidence about school culture, not about individual teachers

Boarding and Residential Life

▼

For boarders, school is also home. There is no end of the day to go home to, harm can continue into evenings and weekends, and a student may be sleeping down the corridor from the person who harmed them.

What the Boarding Standards Ask For

National Minimum Standards for Boarding Schools (2026) (standard 8.4) asks boarding schools' safeguarding policies to reflect four risks, all relevant to misogyny:

  • Child-on-child abuse in shared overnight accommodation, reflecting the unique nature of boarding
  • The school's policy on sexual relationships between children, and that boarders understand it
  • Significant gender imbalance in the school, a house or a year group
  • Devices and harmful online content: devices brought in, content already downloaded, and 3G, 4G and 5G access that bypasses the school's filtering and monitoring

Men and Boys: Responsibility and Role

▼

Ending misogyny is not only a task for women and girls. Men and boys are part of the solution: as role models, as bystanders who act, and as people who can themselves be harmed.

Responsibility, Not Blame

KCSIE 2026 is clear that girls are more likely to be the victims of sexual harassment and sexual violence, and that it is more likely to be perpetrated by boys. The RSHE statutory guidance is equally clear that most boys and young men are respectful to girls, young women and each other, that anyone can be affected by sexual violence, and that staff should avoid language which suggests boys or men are always perpetrators. Students should have opportunities to identify and learn from positive male role models. Where misogynistic ideas are expressed, challenge the ideas, not the person.

Men on the Staff

  • Model it: respectful language and conduct towards women and girls, in lessons, corridors, the staffroom and online
  • Don't leave it to women: challenge sexist comments from students and colleagues yourself, if it is safe to do so. Silence can be read as agreement
  • Don't laugh along: staffroom or sports-team "banter" sets the tone students copy
  • Back up female colleagues: reinforce their authority rather than taking over; follow up privately to check they are OK
  • Take reports seriously: listen, record and pass on to the DSL, in the same way as any other safeguarding concern
  • Reflect: notice your own assumptions, for example who you ask to speak in class, whose behaviour you excuse, and how you describe girls and boys
  • Lead prevention: volunteer to deliver or co-deliver healthy relationships work. Boys benefit from hearing it from men
  • Raise concerns about colleagues: sexist conduct towards colleagues goes to HR. Any conduct that raises a concern about how a colleague behaves with students is reported under KCSIE Part 4, as a low-level concern or an allegation

What We Ask of Boys

Expectations

  • Treat everyone with respect, in person and online
  • Understand and respect consent
  • Never make, share or forward sexual images, "rating" lists or rumours
  • Think critically about online content that presents women as inferior or as owing men anything
  • Be an upstander: if it is safe, say something; if not, tell a trusted adult afterwards
  • Look out for friends who are being drawn into harmful content or behaviour

What We Offer Boys

  • Positive male role models and space to talk about masculinity, pressure, friendship and loneliness
  • Education that explains how misogyny harms boys and young men too
  • Support when they are harmed: in the Ofsted review, 74% of boys said sexist name-calling and 40% said pressure to provide sexual images happens to them or their peers
  • Help if they are worried about their own thoughts or behaviour: Shore, the Lucy Faithfull Foundation's anonymous service for teenagers (shorespace.org.uk)
  • Consequences that are clear and fair, alongside education, so they can change
  • Self-guided student guides written for boys and girls: the Student Guide 101 - 11 to 14 and Student Guide 101 - 14 to 18

Talking with Boys

Helpful

  • "I'm not saying you're a bad person. I'm saying that comment causes harm."
  • "Most boys here don't think like that. You can be one of the people who changes it."
  • "What do you think it's like for her when that's said?"
  • "Where did you come across that idea? Let's look at it together."
  • "If you ever feel you can't say something in front of your mates, you can tell me afterwards."

Unhelpful

  • "All men are..." or "Boys are the problem"
  • Labels such as "toxic masculinity" aimed at an individual
  • Treating every boy as a potential perpetrator
  • Mocking the student, or naming specific influencers (the RSHE guidance advises against signposting content producers)
  • Dismissing boys' own experiences of harassment or pressure

For Leaders

  • Make sure men visibly lead and take part in prevention work, not only women
  • Include male staff explicitly in expectations to challenge sexism among colleagues and students
  • Give boys positive leadership roles in respect and wellbeing work, alongside girls
  • Hear boys' voices in student surveys and act on what they say, including their own experiences of harm
  • Check whether sanctions and support are applied consistently to boys and girls

Identity and Intersecting Harm

▼

Misogyny rarely arrives alone. It is often combined with racism, religious hatred, disablism, homophobia or hostility towards gender-questioning students, and each makes the other worse. The response must not be diluted, and must not be applied unequally.

The Starting Point

KCSIE 2026 asks all staff to be aware that children with special educational needs and disabilities (SEND), children who are, or are perceived to be, lesbian, gay or bisexual, and children who are questioning their gender may face additional vulnerability to bullying and abuse. It is clear that bullying, for whatever reason, is never acceptable (paras 249 to 259). Record every element of an incident: if it was sexist and racist, record both.

Race and Ethnicity

  • Sexualised racism: slurs and "jokes" that combine race and sex, racialised stereotypes about girls' bodies or behaviour, and harassment of girls who wear religious dress such as the hijab.
  • Adultification: Black girls in particular are more likely to be seen as older, more knowing and less in need of protection. Ask yourself whether you would see the same child as vulnerable if she were white.
  • Fairness in sanctions: boys from some racially minoritised groups are more likely to be seen as a risk rather than at risk, and are over-represented in suspensions and exclusions. Check that responses to the same behaviour are consistent.
  • Online content that mixes misogyny with racism or antisemitism needs the same challenge as content that is only misogynistic.

Firmin et al. (2026), pp. 9, 22 and 94.

Culture and Faith

  • Use the guidance in Cultural Considerations (Engage): respect different norms, but never let fear of seeming culturally insensitive stop you acting.
  • Faith is neither an excuse for harm nor a reason for suspicion. Pupils and staff have rights in relation to religion or belief, and the school will still sanction bullying and harassment (KCSIE 2026, para 279).
  • Where so-called honour-based abuse or forced marriage is possible, speak to the DSL straight away and do not contact the family or community first.
  • Offer independent interpreters. Do not use siblings, friends or community members to interpret a disclosure.

Disability and SEND

  • Disabled children are three times more likely to be abused than their peers (KCSIE 2026). Disabled girls can face misogyny and disablism together.
  • Don't put signs of harm down to the condition. KCSIE warns that changes in behaviour, mood or injury may be assumed to relate to a child's needs without further exploration.
  • Communication barriers can stop a child telling you, or recognising that what is happening is abuse. Use their usual communication methods, visual supports and extra time, and check understanding.
  • Fact and fiction online: KCSIE notes some children may find it hard to tell fact from fiction in online content, and repeat it at school. A student with SEND repeating misogynistic content may need explicit teaching, not only a sanction.
  • Involve the SENCO. KCSIE expects close liaison between the DSL and the SENCO where a report involves a child with SEND (para 250), whether they have been harmed or have caused harm.

KCSIE 2026, paras 249 to 251 and 532.

Sexuality

  • Being lesbian, gay or bisexual is not in itself a risk factor, but these students can be vulnerable to discriminatory bullying, and a student who is perceived to be lesbian, gay or bisexual can be just as vulnerable (KCSIE 2026, para 253).
  • Homophobia polices masculinity: "gay" and "girl" used as insults punish boys who show emotion or don't fit the "alpha" mould. Challenge both the homophobia and the misogyny underneath it.
  • Lesbian and bisexual girls may face sexualised harassment about their sexuality.
  • Treat information about a student's sexuality as sensitive. Don't share it beyond what is needed to keep them safe; talk to the DSL before anyone else is told, including parents.

Gender-Questioning Students

  • KCSIE asks schools to stay aware of the potential vulnerabilities of children who are questioning their gender, including discriminatory bullying (para 257), and to be respectful places where bullying is never tolerated (para 259).
  • Harassment of a gender-questioning or trans student, or of a student perceived to be, is child-on-child abuse and must be challenged like any other. The RSHE statutory guidance is clear that people with the protected characteristic of gender reassignment have protection from discrimination and should be treated with respect and dignity (para 69).
  • This guide is about harassment and abuse. Requests relating to social transition follow the school's policy and KCSIE 2026, and are not decided by individual staff.

Fairness Check (Pause)

  • Would I respond in the same way if the students involved were of a different race, sex, faith or sexuality, or did not have SEND?
  • Is anything about the victim's identity making me more, or less, likely to believe them?
  • Is the sanction consistent with what other students have received for similar behaviour?
  • Leaders: review incident and sanction data by group each term, and act on any pattern.

Quick Reference Card

▼

Immediate Danger Comes First

Immediate danger or possible risk to life: call 999 when it is needed, stay with the child, and summon the DSL or a deputy. Don't delay action to work through the steps. The 90-second Pause is for once immediate safety is secured, to stop an impulsive or investigative response.

If You Observe Misogynistic Behaviour

  1. ✅ If it is safe to do so, interrupt and clearly name the issue: "That language/behaviour is misogynistic"
  2. ✅ If it is not safe: do not confront. Get another adult or use on-call; call 999 if anyone is in immediate danger
  3. ✅ Ensure safety of targeted student: Move them away from the situation if it is safe to do so
  4. ✅ Note exact language/actions: Verbatim quotes essential
  5. ✅ Report to the DSL as soon as is practicably possible: straight away if a child is at risk
  6. ✅ Document factually: Date, time, who, what, where, impact

If a Student Discloses Harassment/VAWG

  1. ✅ Thank them for telling you: "Thank you for trusting me with this"
  2. ✅ Believe them and take it seriously: "I believe you, and I'm taking what you've told me seriously"
  3. ✅ Use TED: Tell me, Explain, Describe. Never ask "why"
  4. ✅ Validate their experience: "This was not your fault"
  5. ✅ Explain you must report to DSL: "I need to report this to keep you safe"
  6. ✅ Don't investigate or ask leading questions: Listen, don't interrogate
  7. ✅ Provide immediate support: Safe space, reassurance, dignity
  8. ✅ Document using their exact words: Direct quotes in quotation marks

RED FLAGS Requiring Immediate Escalation

  • 🚨 Threats of violence or sexual violence
  • 🚨 Evidence of organised harassment campaign
  • 🚨 Support for, or intent to commit, violence against women and girls (including glorifying incel-related attacks)
  • 🚨 Nudes or semi-nudes involving a child (do not view, copy or forward)
  • 🚨 Indicators of adult grooming/exploitation
  • 🚨 Student at immediate risk of harm
  • 🚨 Serious sexual assault/violence
  • 🚨 So-called honour-based abuse concerns

Call DSL immediately. If unavailable and risk is immediate, call 999.

What NOT to Do

  • ❌ Don't minimise as "banter" or "just joking"
  • ❌ Don't investigate yourself
  • ❌ Don't search phones yourself, or view, copy or forward nude or semi-nude imagery; follow DSL direction for preserving other online evidence
  • ❌ Don't contact parents before consulting DSL
  • ❌ Don't promise confidentiality
  • ❌ Don't victim-blame ("What were you wearing?")
  • ❌ Don't use "boys will be boys" language
  • ❌ Don't delay reporting to DSL

Online Evidence: One Rule

  • Ordinary messages or posts a student has already shown you: note what is relevant (platform, date, who, exact words) in your record. Don't ask to see more or scroll through a phone, especially where messages may be sexual, because they may contain images. Don't log in to accounts, search profiles or investigate, and never transfer anything to a personal device.
  • Nude or semi-nude images of anyone under 18: don't intentionally view, copy, screenshot, forward, save or delete them. Tell the DSL straight away. If you see one by accident, tell the DSL, ask for support if you need it, and record that it was seen and what you did, without describing the image (UKCIS).
  • The DSL decides what happens to devices and content.
